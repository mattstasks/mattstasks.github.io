## Project Background
The ITD224 Applied Data Science Project focuses on supporting a movie‑streaming platform that operates in the U.S. market by using data science to guide recommendation, catalog design and content licensing decisions. The platform’s leadership faces three related business challenges. First, users are overwhelmed by large catalogues and struggle to discover relevant content, which undermines engagement and watch time. Second, content licensing is costly and risky; without analytical support, the platform may over‑invest in low‑return titles and under‑invest in high‑potential ones. Third, a catalogue dominated only by recent blockbusters is not sufficient for long‑term differentiation; the platform needs to curate a mix that includes enduring “classics” with lasting cultural and audience relevance.

To address these issues, the team defined three business objectives that correspond to three analytical workstreams. Objective 1, led by Lim Ting Wei, is to improve user engagement and content discovery by providing personalized movie recommendations based on content similarity and user behaviour. This objective draws on text analytics, TF‑IDF and cosine similarity, as well as association rule mining to surface “movies similar to this” and “you may also like” suggestions. Objective 2, led by Tan Yan Ling Michelle, is to maximise return on content licensing spend by developing supervised regression models that predict movie ROI from historical metadata, allowing the platform to rank titles according to their expected financial performance. Objective 3, led by Matthew Lim, is to build a well‑rounded catalogue with potential “classics” by identifying movies that exhibit characteristics associated with enduring relevance and constructing classification models that estimate the likelihood that a movie is classic‑like. Together, these objectives form a coherent decision‑support framework: the recommendation system drives engagement; the ROI model improves licensing efficiency; and the “classics” model supports strategic catalogue curation.

### Objective 1: Content‑Based and Behavioural Recommendation System
Data Preparation
For Objective 1, the primary datasets are movies.csv and ratings.csv from a MovieLens‑style Kaggle release, which include movie metadata and user ratings respectively. The movies.csv file contains at least movieId, title and genres, while ratings.csv contains userId, movieId, rating and timestamp. The first step was to filter the ratings to focus on positive user feedback. Ratings with values greater than or equal to 4.0 were treated as implicit likes, in line with prior work that treats high ratings as positive signals when constructing recommender training data.

To control dimensionality and avoid overwhelming the association rule mining algorithm with extremely sparse items, the team computed the number of ratings per movieId and retained only movies with at least 3,000 ratings. This popularity threshold ensures that each retained movie has sufficient behavioural data to contribute meaningful patterns. From the filtered ratings, the team constructed user transactions by grouping each user’s positively rated movieIds into a list. Conceptually, each transaction is analogous to a basket in market basket analysis; in the context of MovieLens 20M, this yields approximately 138,000 user transactions when positive ratings and popularity thresholds are applied. These transactions form the basis for frequent pattern mining and association rule generation.

In parallel, the team prepared textual data for content‑based similarity. Using movies.csv and, where available, additional movie information datasets that provide overviews and taglines, they created a text field that concatenates titles, genres and descriptions for each movie. This text field underwent a basic NLP cleaning pipeline: it was lower‑cased; punctuation and bracketed content were removed using regular expressions; tokens were generated via an NLP library; stopwords were removed; and tokens were lemmatized so that inflected forms such as “running” or “cats” reduce to “run” and “cat”. The team also experimented with part‑of‑speech filtering focused on nouns and proper nouns, since these carry much of the semantic information in short movie descriptions.

For mapping between numeric IDs and human‑readable titles, the team created dictionaries movieid_to_title and title_to_movieid. These mappings are crucial for user interaction: users type titles, the system translates them to IDs for model lookup, and final recommendations are presented back as readable titles.

### Modelling
The recommendation modelling for Objective 1 has two strands: content‑based recommendation using TF‑IDF and sentence transformers, and behavioural recommendation using association rule mining.

On the content‑based side, the team represented each movie as a vector using TF‑IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) applied to the cleaned text field. TF‑IDF weights terms based on how often they appear in a specific document relative to their frequency across the entire corpus, emphasizing words that are distinctive for particular movies. Genres were encoded using a multi‑label binarizer and concatenated with the TF‑IDF vectors, ensuring that genre similarity contributes alongside textual similarity. Cosine similarity, a standard metric for high‑dimensional sparse vectors, was then used to measure similarity between movie vectors; two movies are considered similar if the angle between their TF‑IDF vectors is small, regardless of the absolute length of the vectors.

To explore deeper semantic representations, the team compared the TF‑IDF baseline with Sentence Transformers, specifically the all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2 model. This model is a pre‑trained sentence embedding network that maps sentences or short paragraphs into 384‑dimensional dense vectors, trained with contrastive learning objectives on large sentence pair datasets. When applied to movie descriptions, these embeddings capture contextual and semantic relations beyond mere word overlap. The team encoded all movies into embeddings and computed cosine similarity in this dense vector space. This allowed a comparison between sparse bag‑of‑words features and dense contextual embeddings in terms of the quality and interpretability of similarity‑based recommendations, consistent with recent work on semantic movie search and vector‑based similarity engines.

On the behavioural side, the team used association rule mining to capture co‑watch patterns. They chose the FP‑Growth algorithm as implemented in PySpark’s pyspark.ml.fpm.FPGrowth class, which is a parallel version of FP‑Growth designed for distributed environments. FP‑Growth builds an FP‑tree from the transaction data and mines frequent itemsets without explicitly generating all candidate combinations, making it more scalable than Apriori for large datasets. The model was configured with an itemsCol pointing to the user transaction list, minSupport initially around 0.01, and minConfidence around 0.2. These settings were later increased (for example, minSupport around 0.03–0.05 and minConfidence around 0.5) to reduce the number of frequent itemsets and rules, which in turn mitigated memory usage and runtime issues in the Colab environment. The FP‑Growth model outputs frequent itemsets and association rules of the form {A} → {B}, along with support, confidence and lift metrics.

Given the interaction design, where users typically input one movie at a time, the team filtered the rules to retain only those whose antecedent contains a single movie ID. This simplifies interpretation and aligns with established practice in hybrid recommender systems that combine collaborative and content‑based signals or combine neural collaborative filtering with association rules. The rules were exported from Spark to a pandas DataFrame for downstream use in the recommendation interface.

### Evaluation
Evaluation of Objective 1 models combined qualitative inspection and quantitative metrics. For the content‑based TF‑IDF model, the team examined the top‑N similar movies returned for several anchor titles. In one example, using The Sixth Sense (1999) as input, the TF‑IDF + cosine similarity model surfaced movies such as The Matrix, The Shawshank Redemption, Silence of the Lambs, American Beauty and Pulp Fiction. These titles share elements of tone, era, or audience appeal, and their co‑occurrence in human‑curated “Top 100” lists suggests that the model captures meaningful semantic similarity. When comparing TF‑IDF with sentence‑transformer embeddings, the team observed that the transformer model often surfaced thematically coherent recommendations even when description wording differed, aligning with reports that SBERT‑style models produce better semantic search and clustering results than sparse vectors for natural language tasks.

For the association rules, evaluation focused on rule metrics and stability. The team monitored the number of rules generated, the distribution of support and confidence, and the relative lift of rules. Rules with high support, confidence and lift are more likely to correspond to strong behavioural patterns, such as “Users who liked The Sixth Sense also liked The Matrix,” which is reminiscent of query recommendation and co‑view patterns noted in parallel FP‑Growth literature. At the same time, the team had to manage technical constraints: FP‑Growth runs on the Spark JVM, and large FP‑trees can exhaust memory, especially within Colab’s finite environment. Frequent Py4J ConnectionRefusedError and Py4JNetworkError exceptions indicated that the Java backend was crashing when the algorithm attempted to build or broadcast large rule sets. By tuning minSupport and minConfidence, restricting to popular movies, and testing on smaller user subsamples, the team identified parameter settings that produce a manageable rule set without sacrificing the most important patterns.

### Objective 2: ROI Prediction for Content Licensing
Data Preparation
Objective 2 aims to maximise return on content licensing spend by predicting movie ROI from pre‑release or quasi pre‑release metadata. The primary dataset is a cleaned movie metadata file (movies-metadata-cleaned-dataset-1900–2025) sourced from Kaggle, which includes variables such as title, budget, revenue, genres, production companies, release dates, runtime and other characteristics.

The first step was to address data quality issues. The team removed entries with missing titles and converted empty lists or placeholders into proper missing values. Negative revenue records, implausible runtimes and other outliers were filtered out. To align the dataset with the business context of U.S. streaming, the team restricted the modelling population to U.S.-origin, English‑language feature films with a minimum budget of USD 100,000 and a runtime of at least 40 minutes. Records with non‑positive revenue were excluded because they prevent meaningful ROI calculation. Numerical missing values were imputed using either the median (for continuous predictors) or zero (for counts or indicator variables), depending on their interpretation.

The target variable is ROI, defined as (Revenue−Budget)/Budget, which measures return relative to investment rather than absolute revenue. Because ROI can be highly skewed, especially with low‑budget films achieving extreme percentage returns, the team applied a log transformation, using log(1+ROI) as the modelling target. This transformation stabilizes variance and aligns the distribution with the assumptions of many regression models. In addition, release date was decomposed into year, month, and quarter features, and binary indicators for key holiday and summer release windows were created to capture potential timing effects. Budget was also log‑transformed to reduce the influence of extreme values while retaining relative differences.

Categorical variables such as genres and production companies were encoded using multi‑hot or one‑hot encoding. For example, each genre became a binary feature indicating whether a movie belongs to that genre, and the top twenty production companies were encoded as separate binary variables. All continuous features were standardized using a scaler fitted on the training set to ensure comparability across predictors.

### Modelling
The modelling framework for ROI prediction followed a supervised regression approach. The team split the dataset into training and test sets in an 80:20 ratio, stratified where necessary to maintain coverage across budget quintiles. Preprocessing steps, including encoding and scaling, were fitted on the training set only and then applied to the test set to avoid leakage.

Four main models were benchmarked: Ridge regression, Random Forest, LightGBM and XGBoost, reflecting the range of algorithms commonly used in movie revenue prediction and general regression tasks. Using five‑fold cross‑validation with folds stratified by budget quintile, the team evaluated each model’s R‑squared and mean error metrics. Ridge regression, being linear, underperformed significantly, suggesting that relationships between predictors and ROI are non‑linear. The tree‑based methods, particularly XGBoost, performed better, with cross‑validated around 0.62, comparable to or better than similar studies that use gradient boosting for movie revenue prediction.

Hyperparameter optimisation for XGBoost was performed using a randomized search over ten predefined parameter combinations. For each combination, the same cross‑validation framework was used. The best configuration achieved a cross‑validated of approximately 0.6202 and a training of 0.6729, implying a modest gap and limited overfitting. The test set, which remained untouched during model selection and tuning, yielded an Rsquared of 0.6199, demonstrating strong consistency between cross‑validation and held‑out performance. While the mean absolute error on the original ROI scale remains large (around 99 percentage points), this is partly due to the volatility of ROI percentages, especially for low‑budget films. For this reason, the team positions the model as an exploratory screening tool rather than a precise forecast.

### Evaluation
Evaluation of the ROI model included standard metrics and detailed diagnostic analysis. The close alignment between cross‑validation and test R‑squared suggests that the model generalizes reasonably well within the training domain and that the cross‑validation framework was appropriately structured. Feature importance plots, based on the fitted XGBoost model, showed that budget, documentary genre indicators and release year contribute substantially to the model’s predictive signal, though the team emphasized that these importances reflect model reliance rather than causal effects.

To assess whether performance differs across segments, the team evaluated the model’s R‑squared by genre group on the held‑out test set. Results varied considerably: for example, TV movies achieved an Rsqaure around 0.74, while animation films had a much lower Rsquare around 0.40. This heterogeneity highlights the importance of subgroup analysis to detect pockets where the model underperforms, aligning with responsible AI recommendations that models be evaluated across observable groups rather than only in aggregate.

Finally, the team converted the predicted log‑ROI back to ROI percentages for business interpretation and ranked the held‑out films accordingly. This ranking illustrates how the model could support content screening by highlighting movies with high predicted ROI proxies, particularly among mid‑budget titles where decisions are less obvious. However, because ROI percentages can be dominated by low‑budget outliers and because the target is a proxy rather than actual licensing margin, the team recommended using the model as one input among several in licensing decisions.

### Objective 3: Classic Movie Identification and Catalogue Design
Data Preparation
Objective 3 focuses on identifying potential classic movies to help build a differentiated, enduring catalogue. The primary datasets are a global movie dataset and a separate classics list (for example, a curated “ultimate classic movies” dataset), both obtained from Kaggle. The global dataset covers a large number of movies and associated metadata, while the classics dataset lists films that have been identified as classics based on critical or cultural criteria.

The first challenge was to merge these datasets. The team attempted to match the classics list to the global dataset using movie titles. An initial title‑based merge yielded 893 matched titles, 99,107 unmatched global movies and 4,021 unmatched classics. To improve matching, the team normalized titles by converting to lowercase, removing punctuation, and trimming whitespace, then created a cleaned key combining title and release year. However, matching on title plus year dramatically reduced the number of matches to only nine, revealing inconsistencies in release year data. Duplicate titles in the global dataset further complicated matching, as the same title could appear multiple times with different metadata. After additional checks using runtime and genre as secondary validation features, the team concluded that a robust join was not feasible within the project timeline.

Instead, they retained the 893 title‑matched records as the operational link between the classic list and global dataset and documented this limitation. A classic_flag variable was added, set to 1 for matched classics and 0 for others. Entries that appeared only in the classics dataset but not in the global dataset were dropped when they represented a very small proportion (less than one percent) of all classics, thereby maintaining focus on movies for which full metadata was available.

### Modelling
The analytical objective for Objective 3 is to identify characteristics associated with classic movies and to build a classification model that predicts the probability that a movie is classic‑like. The team framed this as a binary supervised classification problem with classic_flag as the target. Candidate features included genres, production companies, release year, runtime and other metadata. Categorical features were encoded into multi‑hot or one‑hot representations, similar to those used in Objective 2, and numerical features were scaled as needed.

The core modelling pipeline involved splitting the data into training and test sets, stratified by classic_flag given the extreme class imbalance (around 0.9 percent of movies labeled as classics). Three models were benchmarked: logistic regression, decision trees and random forests. Class weights were set to “balanced” to place higher emphasis on the minority classic class. Logistic regression was chosen as a baseline due to its interpretability and suitability for binary classification; decision trees and random forests were included to capture non‑linear relationships and interactions between features.

Despite these efforts, all models struggled. ROC‑AUC scores hovered around 0.50, indicating that the classifiers performed only slightly better than random guessing. Logistic regression achieved higher recall and F1‑score for the classic class than the tree‑based models but suffered from very low precision (around 0.9 percent), reflecting a large number of false positives. The limited predictive performance suggests that the available metadata features did not strongly differentiate classic movies from non‑classics and that the heavy class imbalance and noisy title matching further degraded signal.

### Evaluation
The key analytical finding for Objective 3 is that, within the available data and feature set, classic status is difficult to predict. Severe class imbalance, limited number of matched classics, and potential label noise from title‑based matching all contribute to this challenge. It is also plausible that classic status depends on factors not present in the datasets, such as long‑term audience behaviour, critical reception, cultural influence and awards, which are not captured by basic metadata alone.

Nonetheless, the team used the logistic regression model to compute a predicted classic probability for all movies, then removed those already labeled as classics and ranked the remaining candidates by decreasing probability. This ranked list can be used as an exploratory tool for content discovery, highlighting movies that, based on their metadata, may share some characteristics with classics. However, given the low discriminative performance, the team emphasized that these probabilities should be treated cautiously and used only alongside other signals such as ROI predictions and human judgement.

## Recommendation and Analysis

Across all three objectives, the project builds an integrated analytics framework for a streaming platform. Objective 1 provides a hybrid recommendation system that combines content-based similarity and association rules on user ratings to support personalized and semantically meaningful recommendations. Objective 2 supplies a regression‑based ROI prediction pipeline that ranks movies by expected financial performance, enabling more efficient allocation of licensing budgets. Objective 3 offers a preliminary classic‑likelihood signal that, while limited, can support exploratory catalogue enrichment focused on enduring content.

Together, these components can be integrated into a decision workflow. For example, when considering candidate movies for licensing, the platform could filter out titles with low predicted ROI, prioritize those with high ROI and classic-likelihood scores, and then use the recommendation system from Objective 1 to simulate how these titles might connect into users’ recommendation pathways. Moreover, understanding genre and ROI patterns from Objective 2 can inform which content clusters to emphasize in recommendations, while the classic identification work from Objective 3 supports long‑term catalogue differentiation by ensuring that the system does not only surface recent, high‑ROI titles but also tent‑pole classics that anchor the platform’s identity.

## AI Ethics
The project engages with several dimensions of AI ethics and responsible data science: privacy, fairness, accuracy, accountability and transparency. These concerns align with the responsible AI risks and controls reflected in the Objective 2 work on ROI prediction, which explicitly considered data selection, pre‑release availability, target measurement, historical bias and automation bias.

On the privacy front, user ratings and watch histories are sensitive behavioural data. While MovieLens‑style datasets are anonymised, a production system would process identifiable user behaviour. The team’s design assumes pseudonymised user IDs and restricts data use to what is necessary for recommendation, consistent with data minimization principles. For ROI and classic modelling, the data are primarily public metadata, but care must still be taken not to inadvertently deanonymize individuals or leak sensitive financial information.

Fairness issues arise in multiple ways. In Objective 1, filtering on popularity and using minimum support thresholds biases the recommendation system toward mainstream, frequently watched movies. Less popular or niche content may never appear in association rules and may be under‑represented in TF‑IDF similarity recommendations if they lack rich descriptions. In Objective 2, predictive performance varies across genres, with some genres achieving much higher Rsquare than others; this uneven performance raises concerns about systematically over‑ or under‑estimating ROI for certain content types. In Objective 3, the imbalance between classics and non‑classics and reliance on partial title matching may embed historical or curatorial biases into the classic flag. The team mitigated these issues by analyzing performance by genre, labeling the ROI target as a proxy rather than a ground truth, and recommending that model outputs be used alongside human judgement.

Accuracy and reliability are central concerns. For Objective 1, both content‑based and behavioural models provide probabilistic suggestions rather than guarantees. TF‑IDF and cosine similarity provide sensible semantic neighbours, but the absence of behavioural data means they may not fully capture individual taste. Association rules can misrepresent causality, as co‑watch patterns are correlations and may reflect general popularity rather than specific taste. For Objective 2, ROI predictions are inherently uncertain and based on proxies; the relatively high mean absolute error underscores the need to treat model outputs as approximate signals rather than precise financial forecasts. Objective 3’s low ROC‑AUC indicates that the classic classifier is not reliable as a standalone tool. In all cases, the team positions models as advisory inputs rather than automated decision makers.

Accountability is addressed through methodological transparency and human-in-the-loop decision frameworks. The team uses version-controlled code in GitHub, documents preprocessing and modelling steps, and maintains separate evaluation sets to guard against overfitting and leakage. For ROI and classic decisions, the team explicitly recommends that models be used as screening tools rather than final arbiters of licensing choices, thereby mitigating automation bias. Responsible AI literature emphasizes that predictive models should augment, not replace, human judgement in strategic contexts, a principle this project adopts.

Transparency is provided through clear explanations of recommendation rationales and model design. In the recommendation system, association rules naturally lend themselves to explanations like “Because you watched A, many users also watched B,” while content-based similarity can be explained via shared genres and descriptions. For ROI and classic predictions, feature importance analyses and subgroup evaluations help stakeholders understand what drives model outputs, and explicit identification of ROI as a proxy target clarifies its limitations. Together, these practices support informed, accountable, and ethical use of the models.


## Source Codes and Datasets
The code and artifacts for this project are stored in two public GitHub repositories. The repository at https://github.com/ChaosOrder99/ITD224-Recommendation-system.git contains the Python notebooks and scripts used for data preparation, content-based similarity modelling, association rule mining with PySpark FP‑Growth, and the hybrid recommendation system interface. It also includes the regression and classification pipelines for Objectives 2 and 3, along with intermediate artefacts such as trained models and evaluation reports, where file size and licensing permit. The repository at https://github.com/mxchelletanx/mxchelletanx.github.io.git hosts the project’s documentation site and presentation materials, including slides summarizing the business context, methodology, results and responsible AI considerations.

The primary datasets include movies.csv and ratings.csv derived from the MovieLens 20M dataset, which contains 20 million ratings on 27,278 movies by 138,493 users between 1995 and 2015. Additional datasets used for ROI and classic modelling as well as exploratory catalogue analyses include the Global Movies Dataset 1950–2026, Movies Info (with overviews and genre tags), Movies Metadata Cleaned 1900–2025, Ultimate Classic Movies with TMDB metadata, and a cinema hall ticket sales and customer behaviour dataset, all sourced from Kaggle and curated lists of recommended movie datasets. By combining these resources with contemporary techniques such as TF‑IDF, sentence transformers, FP‑Growth, gradient boosting and logistic regression, the project demonstrates how applied data science can support both operational and strategic decisions in a modern streaming platform.
